\title{An AI-Ready Air-to-Ground Channel Model for HAPS--UAV Access Networks\\
\large Channel-Model Implementation and Signal-Quality Characterisation}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Harsha Matadhikari}
\IEEEauthorblockA{IIT Delhi Online PG Diploma Advanced Comm Engg with Quantum AI Integration}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}

This paper presents a simulation and validation
study for an AI-driven HAPS–UAV–Ground access network,
addressing challenges like SINR heterogeneity induced by a fixed
3-HAPS equilateral-triangle constellation across its service foot-
print. Following Arani et al., we implement (i) a free-space model
for the High-Altitude Platform Station (HAPS) link, (ii)
building-blockage line-of-sight (LoS) probability series for the
Unmanned-Aerial-Vehicle (UAV) links, (iii) average
path loss and downlink signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio
(SINR) under this constellation geometry, and (iv) comprehensive
ITU-R atmospheric losses (rain, fog, gas, scintillation) for feeder-
link design. The channel model is realised in Python (NumPy +
Matplotlib + itur package) and characterised through signal-
quality curves, spatial-heterogeneity analysis, and atmospheric
sensitivity results.
\end{abstract}

HAPS, UAV, non-terrestrial networks, air-to-ground channel, LoS probability, SINR, multi-node networks, UAV relay, feeder link, beam tracking, rain attenuation, system geometry
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Platform Overview}

A typical HAPS system consists of a stratospheric, solar-powered platform operating at approximately 20 km altitude. At this altitude, it maintains a fixed service footprint while undergoing continuous circular flight and attitude variations (roll, pitch, and yaw), supporting a service radius of up to 100 km~\cite{itu_m2101,itu_m2106}. It communicates with ground equipment through

\begin{itemize}
  \item {Feeder-link: HAPS to Gateway} \\
    Feeder link provides high-capacity backhaul between the Ground Gateway (GW) and the HAPS aircraft. Feeder links operate in the 38.0--39.5 GHz Ka-band 3GPP NTN~\cite{3gpp_ts38821}.

  \item { Service link:  HAPS to User} \\
 Service links are designed for connectivity from HAPS directly to  UE, ensuring accessibility without specialized hardware for a long range. Service links operate in the 2--2.2 GHz FDD band~\cite{3gpp_ts38821}.

  \item {Relay Link: HAPS to UAV to User} \\ 
  Relay links have two legs. The first leg connects the HAPS to the UAV, and the second leg connects the UAV to the ground UEs with the UAV positioned at a lower altitude. Relay links serve as the primary backup path for users when direct HAPS-to-UE service links are unavailable. Relay links operate in 38.0--39.5 GHz Ka-band 3GPP NTN~\cite{3gpp_ts38821} for HAPS-UAV leg and the 2-2.2 GHz FDD band~\cite{3gpp_ts38821} for UAV-User leg.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Challenges and Impacts on Connectivity}
\subsubsection{Atmospheric Losses}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The 38.0--39.5 GHz link (Ka-band) incurs significant atmospheric loss from rain (~8--18 dB at 10--25 mm/h per ITU-R P.838~\cite{itu_p838}), fog~\cite{itu_p840}, and gas absorption~\cite{itu_p676}. The 38 GHz band is still chosen as it offers practical spectrum allocation and manageable link margins avoiding the 22.235 GHz water-vapor absorption resonance~\cite{itu_p676} and the 60 GHz oxygen peak~\cite{itu_p676}.
  \item The 2 GHz link exhibits high resilience to atmospheric effects according to ITU-R P.618~\cite{itu_p618}, providing a stable ``last-mile'' connection even under adverse weather. But it is sensitive to building blockage, which can cause significant path loss.
\end{itemize}
\subsubsection{Elevation Angle and Slant Path}
\begin{itemize}
\item[] Because the HAPS provides coverage up to a radius of 100 km~\cite{itu_m2106}, many links operate at low elevation angles. These "slant paths" (§2.1, worst-case 102 km) result in a significantly longer signal trajectory through the moisture-dense troposphere compared to vertical (nadir) paths, leading to higher cumulative attenuation per ITU-R P.618~\cite{itu_p618}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Problem Statement}
The above considerations define the control-layer problem for relay links: determining the position of each UAV relay and assigning service or relay channels so that users are served fairly and reliably. The system must also select the best serving HAPS for either the HAPS-to-UAV or HAPS-to-UE path to maximize throughput and minimize outage. The control layer must adapt to changing user positions, weather conditions, and HAPS battery states. 

In this document, we analyze channel characteristics and determine optimal HAPS service-node selection and UAV placement using a closed-form, rule-based policy, and then explore AI-driven policies to improve performance.

\subsection{Feeder Link Geometry}
Feeder Links operate in the 38.0–39.5 GHz millimeter-wave band allocated to HAPS by ITU-R M.2101 (Ka-band)~\cite{itu_m2101}.
Feeder link is a short-range connection from HAPS aircraft to the ground-based Gateway (Backhaul connection).
\begin{itemize}
  \item Gateway Altitude: 50 m AGL   
  \item Altitude difference: $20\,\mathrm{km} - 0.05\,\mathrm{km} = 19.95\,\mathrm{km}$
  \item Horizontal distance: $50\,\mathrm{m}$ from HAPS nadir.
  \item Elevation angle: $\theta = \arctan\left(\frac{19.95}{0.05}\right) \approx 89.86^\circ$ (near zenith)
  \item Slant path: $L_{\text{slant}} = \sqrt{19.95^2 + 0.05^2} \approx 19.95\,\mathrm{km}$
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Relay Link Geometry}

The relay link (HAPS-to-UAV 38.0–39.5 GHz and UAV-to-User, f = 2.1 -- 2.2 GHz~\cite{itu_m2101}) connects HAPS to ground users via the UAV relay node positioned at
500 m AGL (Table~\ref{tab:config}).
\subsubsection{HAPS-to-UAV leg (38 GHz)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item UAV Relay Altitude: 500 m AGL  
  \item Altitude difference: $20\,\mathrm{km} - 0.5\,\mathrm{km} = 19.5\,\mathrm{km}$
  \item Horizontal distance: $500\,\mathrm{m}$ from HAPS nadir.
  \item Elevation angle: $\theta = \arctan\left(\frac{19.5}{0.5}\right) \approx 88.5^\circ$ (near zenith)
  \item Slant path: $L_{\text{slant}} = \sqrt{19.5^2 + 0.5^2} \approx 19.51\,\mathrm{km}$
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{UAV-to-User leg (2.1 -- 2.2 GHz)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item User Altitude: 1.5 m (hand-held)  
  \item Altitude difference: $0.5\,\mathrm{km} - 0.0015\,\mathrm{km} = 0.4985\,\mathrm{km}$
  \item Horizontal distance: $500\,\mathrm{m}$ to $100\,\mathrm{km}$ from UAV.
  \item Elevation angle: For 500m horizontal distance from UAV, $\theta = \arctan\left(\frac{0.4985}{0.5}\right) \approx 44.9^\circ$, for larger distances, the angle becomes much smaller.
  \item Slant path: $L_{\text{slant}} = \sqrt{0.4985^2 + 0.5^2} \approx 0.707\,\mathrm{km}$
\end{itemize}

The second leg of relay link is dominated by
horizontal distance and building blockage rather than altitude. The attenuation grows sharply once
the horizontal offset exceeds the LoS-to-NLoS transition set by local building density and
built-up ratio (\S III.C, Eq.~\eqref{eq:los}--\eqref{eq:losJ}). This is why the UAV relay's
placement -- altitude and horizontal offset from served users -- depends on local landscape/building statistics. .

\subsection{Service Link Geometry}

service radius. Although the service link shares the 2 GHz band with the relay link, its power budget
 must close over a much longer range with a mostly-unobstructed, near-zenith-dominated geometry.

\section{System and Channel Model}
% =====================================================================

This section presents three channel models for HAPS to Ground Scenario: (A) generic propagation formulae applicable to all frequency bands, (B) the 38 GHz feeder link model, and (C) the 2 GHz service/relay link model.

% =====================================================================
\subsection{Common System Models (All Frequency Bands)}
% =====================================================================

\subsubsection{Free-space path loss}
FSPL is calculated on the basis of a 20 km vertical zenith distance and a lateral reach extending to 100 km.
The ITU-R P.530~\cite{itu_p530} free-space path loss formula FSPL(MHz, km) is defined as
\begin{equation}
L_\mathrm{FSPL} = 32.44 + 20\log_{10} f_\mathrm{MHz} + 20\log_{10} d_\mathrm{km} \;[\mathrm{dB}].
\label{eq:fspl}
\end{equation}
\noindent\textbf{Equation (1) --- Free-Space Path Loss~\cite{itu_p530}}: $f_\mathrm{MHz}$ is the frequency of the HAPS carrier in MHz and $d_\mathrm{km}$ is the distance in km. This formula applies for all frequencies and distances in the HAPS service footprint, establishing the baseline attenuation before atmospheric and propagation effects. Used in both feeder-link (38 GHz, §III.B) and service-link (2 GHz, §III.C) budgets.

% =====================================================================
\subsection{38 GHz Feeder Link Model (HAPS-to-Gateway Backhaul)}
% =====================================================================

The feeder link (HAPS to Ground Gateway/UAV Relay, 38--39.5 GHz Ka-band per ITU-R M.2101). This path is vulnerable to atmospheric attenuation from rain, fog, gas absorption, and scintillation. Section III.B models each component sequentially.

\subsubsection{Rain attenuation (ITU-R P.618~\cite{itu_p618}, P.838~\cite{itu_p838})}
Rain attenuation $\gamma_R$ (dB/km) is calculated from the local rain
rate $R$ (mm/h) using the empirical ITU-R P.838 coefficients~\cite{itu_p838} $k,\alpha$:
\begin{equation}
\gamma_R = k(f,\theta) \; R^{\alpha(f,\theta)} \quad [\mathrm{dB/km}],
\label{eq:rain_spec_atten}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Equation (2) --- Specific Rain Attenuation~\cite{itu_p838}}: For 38 GHz, typical coefficients are $k=0.0318$ and $\alpha=0.921$ per ITU-R P.838 Annex 1~\cite{itu_p838}. These values are interpolated from Table 1 (P.838 Annex 1) and represent the power-law relationship between the rain rate and attenuation.

When we multiply the effective path length $L_\mathrm{eff}$ (km) by this, we
obtain the rain loss:
\begin{equation}
A_\mathrm{rain} = \gamma_R \; L_\mathrm{eff} \quad [\mathrm{dB}],
\label{eq:rain_loss}
\end{equation}


\noindent\textbf{Equation (3) --- Total Rain Attenuation~\cite{itu_p618}}: $L_\mathrm{eff}$ is computed according to ITU-R P.618~\cite{itu_p618} (slant-path reduction factors and
site-dependent scaling for non-uniform rain along the path). For the worst-case feeder link (102 km slant, 11.3$^\circ$ elevation),
$L_\mathrm{eff} \approx 66$ km with reduction factor $r(\theta) \approx 0.65$, which yields an attenuation of approximately \textbf{5.9 dB at 10 mm/h} and \textbf{15.2 dB at 25 mm / h}~\cite{itu_p618}.

\subsubsection{Clouds and fog (ITU-R P.840~\cite{itu_p840})}
Fog attenuation depends on the liquid-water density $M$ [g/m$^3$], frequency
$f$, and temperature $T$ per ITU-R P.840~\cite{itu_p840}. It is negligible below about 10 GHz, becomes noticeable
in the millimeter-wave bands, and can be several dB in dense fog.
Typical values are approximately 0.05 g/m$^3$ for medium fog and 0.5 g/m$^3$ for
dense fog~\cite{itu_p840}.
Cloud/fog attenuation is modeled as a specific attenuation $\gamma_c$ (dB/km)
times the slant-path length $L_\mathrm{slant}$ (km):
\begin{equation}
A_\mathrm{cloud} = \gamma_c(f,M,T) \;L_\mathrm{slant} \quad [\mathrm{dB}],
\label{eq:fog_loss}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Equation (4) --- Fog/Cloud Attenuation~\cite{itu_p840}}: Specific attenuation $\gamma_c$ depends on frequency and liquid-water-content per ITU-R P.840~\cite{itu_p840}.
where $\gamma_c$ depends on the liquid-water density $M$ [g/m$^3$], frequency
and, weakly, on temperature. Typical liquid-water densities are $M\approx0.05$ for
medium fog and $M\approx0.5$ for dense fog; $\gamma_c$ is small below 10 GHz and
grows into the millimeter-wave bands~\cite{itu_p840}. For the 38 GHz feeder link,
fog contributes negligibly to total loss (typically $<$0.2 dB).

\subsubsection{Atmospheric gases (ITU-R P.676~\cite{itu_p676})}
Atmospheric gas attenuation combines oxygen and water-vapour specific
attenuations $\gamma_o$ and $\gamma_w$ (dB/km) computed from 
local pressure $p$, temperature $T$ and water-vapour density $\rho_w$ according to ITU-R P.676~\cite{itu_p676}.
The total gas loss is
\begin{equation}
A_\mathrm{gas} = (\gamma_o(p,T,f) + \gamma_w(\rho_w,T,f))\;L_\mathrm{slant} \quad [\mathrm{dB}],
\label{eq:gas_loss}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Equation (5) --- Atmospheric Gas Absorption~\cite{itu_p676}}: Combines oxygen and water-vapor components according to ITU-R P.676 Sections 3.1 and 3.2~\cite{itu_p676}. The strongest absorption lines occur at 22.235 GHz and 183 GHz for water
vapor, and near 59.8 GHz and 118.75 GHz for oxygen~\cite{itu_p676} (P.676 Sect. 3.1--3.2, Figure 2).
This makes Ka/Q/V-band HAPS feeder bands much more sensitive to moisture than the sub-6 GHz access links.
At 38 GHz, clear-sky gas absorption is approximately 0.2--0.3 dB/km, orders of magnitude lower than
at the 60 GHz oxygen resonance ($\sim$15--20 dB/km), justifying the 38 GHz band choice per ITU-R analysis~\cite{itu_p676}.

\subsubsection{Tropospheric scintillation (ITU-R P.618\texorpdfstring{\cite{itu_p618}}{[ITU-R P.618]})}
Tropospheric scintillation refers to rapid fluctuations in signal amplitude and
phase caused by small-scale refractive-index irregularities in the lower
troposphere per ITU-R P.618~\cite{itu_p618}. In Ka-band (around 38 GHz) feeder links, scintillation can cause
dynamic fading even in clear-sky conditions and must be taken into account when
designing beam-tracking and link-budget margins~\cite{itu_p618}.

The fade depth due to scintillation for a specific time percentage $p$ is
commonly expressed as short-term amplitude fluctuations using the time percentage model (ITU-R P.618, Annex 1~\cite{itu_p618}):
\begin{equation}
A_\mathrm{scint}(p) = a(p) \cdot \sigma_{pre}(f,\theta,\mathrm{climate}) \quad [\mathrm{dB}],
\label{eq:scint_loss}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Equation (6) --- Scintillation Fade Depth~\cite{itu_p618}}: where $a(p)$ maps standard-deviation values to time-percentage fades (ITU-R P.618 Annex 1~\cite{itu_p618})
and $\sigma_{pre}$ is calculated from the frequency, elevation angle $\theta$ and
local turbulence parameters in the recommendation. We then scale by $a(p)$ to obtain fade depths
for availability analyses and margin setting in adaptive systems. For 38 GHz at 11.3$^\circ$ elevation
and 0.01% outage, typical scintillation fade is 2--4 dB~\cite{itu_p618}.

So total slant-path attenuation for a feeder link may be written as
\begin{equation}
L_\mathrm{total} = L_\mathrm{FSPL} + A_\mathrm{rain} + A_\mathrm{cloud/fog}
                  + A_\mathrm{gas} + A_\mathrm{scintillation},
\label{eq:feeder_loss}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Equation (7) --- Total Feeder Link Attenuation~\cite{itu_p618}}: This equation sums all atmospheric and propagation effects (Eqs. 1--6) to compute the worst-case link budget for the HAPS-to-Gateway feeder at 38 GHz. The breakdown is: FSPL (Eq. 1) + rain (Eq. 3) + fog (Eq. 4) + gas (Eq. 5) + scintillation (Eq. 6), per ITU-R P.618 design guidance~\cite{itu_p618}.

\subsection{2 GHz Service/Relay Link Model (UAV-to-User Air-to-Ground)}
% =====================================================================

Service and relay links operate at 2--2.2 GHz (S-band per 3GPP TS 38.821), connecting ground users either directly to HAPS (service link, high elevation angle) or via UAV relays at 500 m altitude (relay link). Unlike the 38 GHz feeder link, the 2 GHz S-band is resilient to atmospheric effects (fog, rain, gas absorption all negligible per ITU-R P.618) but highly sensitive to building blockage. This section models LoS probability, path loss, and multi-node interference.

\subsubsection{UAV air-to-ground link: LoS probability}
For a UAV $u$ at horizontal distance $r_{u,k}$ from user $k$, the LoS
probability follows the exact building-blockage product series
of~\cite{arani2023}, originally attributed to~\cite{holispechac2008} (IEEE Trans. Antennas Propag., 2008, vol. 56, no. 4, pp. 1078--1084):
\begin{equation}
P^\mathrm{LoS}_{u,k} = \prod_{n=0}^{J}\left[1-\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2\xi^2}
\Big(h_u-\tfrac{n+\frac12}{J+1}(h_u-h_k)\Big)^{2}\right)\right],
\label{eq:los}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Equation (8) --- Building-Blockage LoS Probability~\cite{holispechac2008}}: Exact product-series model~\cite{holispechac2008} for line-of-sight probability accounting for cumulative building blockage along the path. The product iterates over building layers from ground to UAV height.

\begin{equation}
J = \left\lfloor \frac{r_{u,k}}{1000}\sqrt{\alpha\beta} - 1 \right\rfloor,
\label{eq:losJ}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Equation (9) --- Number of Building Layers~\cite{holispechac2008}}: Computed from horizontal distance $r_{u,k}$ (meters), building density $\beta$ [buildings/km$^2$], and built-up ratio $\alpha$ [dimensionless] per Holis \& Pechac Table I~\cite{holispechac2008}.

\noindent Parameters from Table I (Holis \& Pechac 2008, IEEE Trans. Antennas Propag.~\cite{holispechac2008}): $P_\mathrm{NLoS}=1-P_\mathrm{LoS}$; $\alpha$ is the built-up land ratio,
$\beta$ the mean building density [buildings/km$^2$], and $\xi$ a building-height parameter [m]. All are
environment-dependent: for urban areas~\cite{holispechac2008},
$\alpha=0.3$, $\beta=500$, $\xi=15$\,m; dense urban uses $\alpha=0.5$, $\beta=300$, $\xi=20$\,m;
suburban $\alpha=0.1$, $\beta=750$, $\xi=8$\,m; high-rise $\alpha=0.5$, $\beta=300$, $\xi=50$\,m (Table I, cited in 300+ subsequent works)~\cite{holispechac2008}.

\noindent\textbf{Landscape-based relay placement:} The LoS probability model above (Eq.~\eqref{eq:los}--\eqref{eq:losJ}), with its environment-dependent $\alpha,\beta,\xi$ parameter sets (urban, dense urban, suburban, high-rise), already provides the mechanism for determining where a UAV relay stays in LoS of its served users. This same mechanism determines the UAV relay's altitude and horizontal-offset placement per landscape, as introduced in \S II (Relay Link Geometry).

\noindent\textbf{Single-relay scope:} This document limits the topology to one UAV relay (Table~\ref{tab:config}), so the interference sum in the SINR expression (Eq.~\eqref{eq:sinr}, generalized as Eq.~\eqref{eq:sinr-multinode}) need only account for the 3 HAPS for the relay link -- no UAV-to-UAV (relay-to-relay) interference term is needed.

\subsubsection{Average path loss and SINR}
Unlike terrestrial base stations, HAPS nodes operate at high elevation angles. This near-zenith positioning provides a superior Line-of-Sight (LoS) probability, significantly reducing multi-path fading and shadowing in urban and mountainous terrain.
Each state $z\in\{\mathrm{LoS},\mathrm{NLoS}\}$ adds a mean excess loss $\eta_z$;
the two are probability-averaged:
\begin{align}
\mathrm{PL}_z &= 20\log_{10}\!\Big(\tfrac{4\pi f d_\mathrm{3D}}{c}\Big) + \eta_z,\\
\overline{\mathrm{PL}} &= P_\mathrm{LoS}\,\mathrm{PL}_\mathrm{LoS}
                        + P_\mathrm{NLoS}\,\mathrm{PL}_\mathrm{NLoS},
\label{eq:pl}
\end{align}

\noindent\textbf{Equation (10) --- Probability-Averaged Path Loss}: The first line computes path loss (free-space FSPL plus environment-dependent excess $\eta_z$) for LoS and NLoS states separately. The second line probability-averages over the LoS state (Eq. 8). The 3D distance is $d_\mathrm{3D}=\sqrt{r^2+(h_u-h_k)^2}$ where $r$ is horizontal distance and $h_u, h_k$ are UAV and user altitudes respectively.

The received power follows the standard link-budget relation
\begin{equation}
P_r = P_t + G_t + G_r - PL(d,\theta,\mathrm{state}),
\label{eq:rx-power}
\end{equation}
where $P_t$ is transmit power, $G_t$ and $G_r$ are the transmit and receive antenna gains, and $PL(d,\theta,\mathrm{state})$ is the path loss for the current geometry and propagation state. A blocked or NLoS link therefore yields a smaller $P_r$ through a larger path-loss term (equivalently, a lower channel gain $g_{u,k}$); it is not added as a separate degradation term in the SINR expression itself. The reduced signal power is already embedded in the SINR numerator before interference and noise are considered.

The downlink SINR is
\begin{equation}
\gamma_{u,k} = \frac{p_u\,g_{u,k}}{\sum_{u'\neq u} p_{u'} g_{u',k} + \sigma_0^2},
\qquad g_{u,k}=10^{-\overline{\mathrm{PL}}/10},
\label{eq:sinr}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Equation (11) --- Signal-to-Interference-plus-Noise Ratio (SINR)}: Computes downlink SINR at user $k$ from UAV $u$ per standard information-theory formulation. $p_u$ is transmit power [Watts], $g_{u,k}=10^{-\overline{\mathrm{PL}}/10}$ is channel gain (reciprocal of path loss in linear scale), interference sums over competing UAVs $u'$, and $\sigma_0^2$ is thermal noise power [Watts]. For the noise-limited characterization below, interference is negligible; the characterisation below is
noise-limited.

\subsection{Multi-HAPS User Association -- State and Baseline Policies}
\label{sec:assoc-baseline-setup}

Building on the 3-HAPS constellation (Table~\ref{tab:config}) and the SINR model of
Sections~III.A--III.C, this section defines a per-user association experiment: each
of $N=50$ users, once per simulated hour, observes its SINR to each of the 3 HAPS
(dB, Eq.~\eqref{eq:sinr}), its horizontal distance to each (km), each HAPS's battery
state of charge (\%), and the current weather (rain rate, visibility, hour of day).
One episode is one simulated day (24 hourly steps). Per episode, user positions and
each HAPS's initial battery SoC ($\mathcal{U}(30,100)\,\%$) are drawn once and held
fixed; weather is drawn once per episode (\texttt{WeatherScenarioGenerator},
\texttt{random\_mix} mode). Each HAPS's battery is charged by a fixed diurnal solar
profile and discharged by a constant platform/feeder draw plus its currently-associated
users' share of load ($P_{k,\text{user}}=1$~W per user, ASSUMED); the solar/platform
terms dominate, so time-of-day remains the primary driver of SoC and the association
policy has a secondary, but non-trivial, effect on it. Rain de-rates only the
feeder-link throughput credit (Section~\ref{subsec:multinode-sinr}), not the 2~GHz
service SINR.

Two closed-form (non-learned) association policies are evaluated here:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item \textbf{Random}: uniform-random platform choice each hour (sanity floor).
  \item \textbf{Max-SINR}: each user always associates to $\arg\max_p \text{SINR}_{k,p}$
    -- pure closed-form physics, no memory of battery or fairness state, no learning.
\end{itemize}
Both are evaluated over 50 held-out episodes (seed 999) using Jain's fairness index,
outage fraction (SINR below $-2$~dB, the severe-outage threshold measured in
Section~IV.B/Table A.3), and aggregate throughput (Shannon capacity, summed over
users) as metrics. Section~\ref{sec:assoc-baseline-results} reports the resulting
comparison; Section~\ref{sec:phase2-motivation} (Phase 2) later adopts this same
per-user state and evaluates whether a learned policy can improve further on the
closed-form baseline established there.

\subsubsection{Scintillation fade depth (Eq.~12)}

Tropospheric scintillation is rapid amplitude fluctuations caused by refractive-index turbulence in the lower atmosphere. It is governed by the frequency-dependent formula as per 


\begin{equation}
D(p, \theta, f) = D_{\mathrm{ref}} \cdot \left(\frac{f}{f_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^{0.7}
                 \cdot \frac{1}{\sin(\theta)} \cdot \sqrt{\log(1/p)} \quad [\mathrm{dB}],
\label{eq:scint_fade}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item $p$ = time percentage (availability criterion, e.g., $p=1\%$ means fade depth exceeded 1\% of the time, or 99\% availability)
  \item $\theta$ = elevation angle from receiver to transmitter [degrees] 
  \item $D_{\mathrm{ref}} = 2.5\,\mathrm{dB}$ = reference fade depth at K-band ($f_{\mathrm{ref}}=20\,\mathrm{GHz}$, $p=1\%$, $\theta=30^\circ$) per ITU-R P.618-13 Annex 2
  \item Exponent $0.7$ is \textbf{empirical}, validated through radiowave propagation measurements
\end{itemize}

The elevation scaling $1/\sin(\theta)$ reflects the longer atmospheric path at lower angles; near-zenith links ($\theta \approx 90^\circ$) have minimal fading.

Figure~\ref{fig:scint_fade_depth} fade depth ratio between 38~GHz and 2~GHz is  $7.854 = (38/2)^{0.7}$ and the db offset is $10 \log_{10} (7.85)=8.95\mathrm{dB}$, confirming the frequency scaling exponent in Equation~\eqref{eq:scint_fade}.

\subsection{38 GHz Feeder Link (Atmospheric and Multi-Node Effects)}
% =====================================================================

\subsubsection{Atmospheric Loss Analysis}

The feeder link (HAPS-to-Gateway, 38 GHz) experiences significant atmospheric attenuation
dominated by rain, with gas, fog, and scintillation as secondary effects. The atmospheric loss is
highly frequency-dependent per ITU-R P.838~\cite{itu_p838}, with catastrophic peaks at 22.235 GHz (water vapor)~\cite{itu_p676} and 59.8 GHz (oxygen)~\cite{itu_p676}.
Rain loss increases monotonically with frequency (P.838~\cite{itu_p838} coefficients $k$, $\alpha$ grow from 10 to 100 GHz),
so lower frequencies inherently have less rain attenuation. However, the feeder link cannot use the service link's 2 GHz band due to regulatory, link-budget, and capacity constraints:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item \textbf{Regulatory separation}: ITU-R allocates 2 GHz (S-band) for \emph{terrestrial mobile access} (3GPP) and 38--39.5 GHz (Ka-band) for \emph{fixed HAPS backhaul} (ITU-R M.2101). Spectrum efficiency and interference isolation require distinct bands.
  \item \textbf{Antenna directivity}: At 2 GHz, wavelength $\lambda = 15$\,cm; a 1 m antenna achieves only $\sim$3.2° beamwidth. At 38 GHz, $\lambda = 7.9$\,mm; same antenna achieves $\sim$0.2° beamwidth, enabling point-to-point backhaul with interference rejection.
  \item \textbf{Link budget over 102 km}: Feeder path loss at 2 GHz would be $\approx$ 138.5 dB (vs. 164 dB at 38 GHz). To meet same receiver power with 2 GHz would require either 25.5 dB higher transmit power (infeasible on aircraft, power-limited) or larger ground antenna array.
  \item \textbf{Backhaul capacity}: Higher frequencies enable wider bandwidth (Ka-band supports 1+ GHz channels); 2 GHz band is contended with terrestrial mobile networks.
\end{itemize}
Thus, 38 GHz is chosen as a \emph{frequency tradeoff}: higher rain loss than 2 GHz, but it avoids resonance peaks at 22.2 GHz and 60 GHz while enabling practical high-gain antennas and spectrum allocation per ITU-R M.2101~\cite{itu_m2101}.

Figure~\ref{fig:atm_frequency_nominal} shows atmospheric loss across 10--100 GHz for the nominal feeder
link as four individual subfigures: (a) Ka-band (10--40 GHz) with
water-vapor peak at 22.2 GHz, (b) Q-band (40--80 GHz) with oxygen peak at 60 GHz, (c) V-band (80--100 GHz)
in continuum region, and (d) full spectrum on log-scale showing relative severity of all peaks. This
frequency dependence is independent of elevation angle; the worst-case feeder link (11.3$^\circ$ elevation,
100 km service edge) exhibits the same frequency profile but with magnitudes roughly doubled due to longer
slant path (102 km vs.\ 54 km nominal), as quantified in Tables~\ref{tab:atm_losses_nominal} and
~\ref{tab:atm_losses_worstcase}.

\subsubsection{Multi-Node Interference and SINR Effects (2~GHz Service Link Only)}

\label{subsec:multinode-sinr}

\textbf{Scope clarification:} This subsection addresses multi-HAPS interference effects that apply \textbf{exclusively to the 2~GHz service link}. The 38~GHz feeder link operates at short range (10--500 m) to a gateway in proximity to the serving HAPS nadir, with interfering HAPS $\sim$65~km away. Consequently, feeder-link interference is negligible (far below noise floor), and the feeder link is \textbf{noise-limited, not interference-limited} (see \S IV.C.1 paragraph ``Why multi-HAPS interference is not applicable to feeder links''). The following SINR formulation and calculations apply to service-link analysis only.

The single-node noise-limited analysis above assumes only one HAPS transmits at a
time. In a practical multi-HAPS network, multiple nodes may transmit simultaneously
on the same frequency, creating mutual interference. Although service links experience a
10--20\,dB SINR penalty from this interference, a 3-HAPS constellation provides critical advantages that
justify the interference trade-off for 2~GHz service-link users.

\paragraph{Multi-Node SINR Formula:}

We adopt the standard multi-node SINR formulation from Arani~\emph{et~al.}~(2023) and ITU-R P.618:

\begin{equation}
\gamma_{u,k} = \frac{P_u \cdot g_{u,k}}{N_0^2 + \sum_{u' \neq u} P_{u'} \cdot g_{u',k}}
\label{eq:sinr-multinode}
\end{equation}

\noindent In dB domain:

\begin{equation}
\text{SINR}_{\text{dB}} = 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{C}{N + I}\right)
\label{eq:sinr-db}
\end{equation}

\noindent Where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $P_u$ = transmit power of serving HAPS $u$ [W]
  \item $g_{u,k} = 10^{-\overline{\mathrm{PL}}(u,k)/10}$ = channel gain from serving HAPS to user $k$ (linear scale)
  \item $N_0^2$ = thermal noise power [W]
  \item $\sum_{u' \neq u} P_{u'} \cdot g_{u',k}$ = aggregated interference power from all other HAPS
  \item $C$ = carrier power [W], $N$ = noise power [W], $I$ = total interference power [W]
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{Critical implementation note:} Calculation must proceed in linear domain (Watts),
then convert result to dB. dB-domain arithmetic (e.g., subtracting SINR values) is not
equivalent to this linear-domain division and will produce incorrect results (Goldsmith 2005).

\paragraph{LoS blockage in path loss -- tier-dependent, not universal:}

As already introduced in Section~III, line-of-sight blockage is not modeled as an additional degradation term in the SINR expression itself. It is embedded in the path-loss model through the effective received power,
\begin{equation}
P_r = P_t + G_t + G_r - PL(d,\theta,\mathrm{state}),
\end{equation}
so a blocked or NLoS link simply corresponds to a larger $PL$ (or lower channel gain $g_{u,k}$), and the reduced signal power is already reflected in the SINR numerator before interference and noise are considered. This distinction is important: the propagation state affects the channel gain, not the algebraic form of the SINR ratio itself. There are two physically distinct tiers:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{HAPS tier (20\,km altitude) -- both the 2\,GHz direct service link and the 38\,GHz
    feeder link}: pure free-space path loss (Eq.~1), \textbf{with no LoS/NLoS blockage term at all},
    per Arani, Hu \& Zhu (2023) Eq.~2~\cite{arani2023}. We adopt Arani et al.'s free-space model for
    consistency with our primary source. This is exact only very close to nadir ($>85^\circ$
    elevation holds within $\sim$2\,km of nadir); at the 11--22$^\circ$ elevations that dominate
    beyond 50\,km, it is a simplification. Notably, Holis \& Pechac (2008) -- the source of the
    $(\alpha,\beta,\xi)$ parameters used below -- was itself written specifically for elevation-dependent
    shadowing \emph{via high-altitude platforms}, not low-altitude links, so it would be a natural
    candidate for modeling HAPS-tier shadowing more precisely; we defer that refinement to future
    work rather than mix it into the present Arani-consistent baseline. Consequently, SINR at this
    tier is \textbf{landscape-independent} in the current model -- suburban, urban, dense-urban, and
    high-rise all produce identical path loss and SINR (see Table~\ref{table:app-arani-table1} and
    Figures~\ref{fig:sinr-service-link},~\ref{fig:sinr-feeder-link}).
  \item \textbf{UAV/relay tier}: building blockage is applied here using the
    same product-series LoS-probability formula and $(\alpha,\beta,\xi)$ parameter table from
    Holis \& Pechac (2008) (Section~III.C, subsection ``UAV air-to-ground link: LoS probability'') --
    borrowed for this lower-altitude tier in the absence of a UAV-specific model in Arani et al.,
    not because the source model was originally derived for UAV altitudes. The average path loss
    is computed as a weighted blend:

\begin{equation}
\overline{\mathrm{PL}} = P_{\mathrm{LoS}} \cdot \mathrm{PL}_{\mathrm{LoS}} + P_{\mathrm{NLoS}} \cdot \mathrm{PL}_{\mathrm{NLoS}}
\label{eq:pathlos-blend}
\end{equation}

\noindent where each term is a function of $(r, h, \text{env})$.

\noindent    where $P_{\mathrm{LoS}}$ is computed from the building-blockage product series (Eq.~3,
    Section~III.C) using environment-specific parameters: $\alpha$ (built-up ratio), $\beta$ (building
    density), and $\xi$ (mean building height). $\mathrm{PL}_{\mathrm{NLoS}}$ is significantly higher
    than $\mathrm{PL}_{\mathrm{LoS}}$ (typically 15--30\,dB), so denser urban environments exhibit
    higher average path loss and lower received signal power \emph{at this tier only}. (Note: the
    $\eta_{\mathrm{los}}$/$\eta_{\mathrm{nlos}}$ constants currently used for this blend are flat dB
    offsets, not yet cross-checked against a dedicated source table -- see the Phase~2 note in
    \texttt{channel\_model.py}.)
\end{itemize}

This design ensures the SINR formula (Equation~\eqref{eq:sinr-multinode}) remains clean and, where
blockage applies, the physics are captured entirely through the antenna-to-antenna channel gain
$g_{u,k}$ -- never as a separate multiplicative penalty in the SINR equation itself.

Now turning to the constellation trade-off:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Coverage redundancy}: If one HAPS fails or drifts, users remain
    connected to at least one other node. Single-node systems have no fallback.
  \item \textbf{100\,km footprint}: A 3-HAPS equilateral triangle ($\sim 60$--$70$\,km
    spacing) covers the full 100\,km service area. Single nodes cover only
    $\sim 50$\,km radius (half the footprint).
  \item \textbf{Coordinated transmission}: TDMA scheduling (each HAPS transmits in
    time slots) or FDMA (frequency division) eliminates simultaneous interference.
    The interference shown in Figure 5 represents the worst case (all 3 nodes
    on same channel); real systems use coordination.
  \item \textbf{Beamforming opportunity}: Directional antennas reduce interference
    by 10--15\,dB, recovering most of the lost SINR without additional spectrum.
  \item \textbf{Load balancing}: Multiple HAPS nodes distribute traffic, reducing
    congestion and per-user power requirements.
\end{itemize}

the resulting spatial SINR pattern it produces at r~=~50\,km: a good sector (away from both
interferers) and a bad sector (toward them), rather than a distance-only falloff.

\paragraph{Why multi-HAPS interference is not applicable to feeder links:}
The key distinction from service-link analysis is that the feeder link operates at extremely short range (0--0.5 km, typically 10--500 m), placing the gateway in \emph{proximity to the nadir} of the serving HAPS. In contrast, the interfering HAPS in the constellation are positioned approximately 65~km away from the serving HAPS (equilateral-triangle geometry, \S III.A). Consequently, the path-loss attenuation to the interfering HAPS is so severe ($\sim$120~dB at 38~GHz over 65~km) that their interference contribution remains \emph{negligibly small} compared to the receiver's thermal noise level ($-100$\,dBm assumed floor). Therefore, the feeder link is \textbf{noise-limited, not interference-limited}, unlike the service link. The resulting SINR degradation is driven primarily by the background receiver noise, not by multi-HAPS interference from the constellation.

A Johnson--Nyquist thermal-noise model, $N=kTB$, or equivalently $N_{\text{dBm}}=-174+10\log_{10}(B)+NF$, would provide a more physically grounded noise estimate than the assumed flat $-100$\,dBm noise floor. However, for this short-range analysis, replacing the assumed noise floor with a bandwidth- and receiver-dependent thermal-noise value would primarily change the absolute SINR level rather than its nearly flat variation with distance. A more detailed link-budget analysis incorporating the actual channel bandwidth, receiver noise figure, directional gateway antenna gain, and HAPS transmit power is therefore deferred to the next phase. 

\paragraph{2~GHz Service Link: Distance dependence}
When averaged over all angular positions relative to the constellation, the mean SINR decreases from $+6.3$\,dB at $r=10$\,km to $+2.0$\,dB at $r=30$\,km, falls below 0\,dB between 30 and 50\,km, and reaches $-4.3$\,dB at $r=100$\,km (Figure~\ref{fig:multinode-interference}). The interference-to-noise ratio (SINR\_Interferers) remains positive, ranging from 0.95 to 4.6\,dB, throughout the 10--100\,km range.

\paragraph{2~GHz Service Link: Angular (constellation-geometry) dependence}
At a given horizontal distance, the SINR exhibits substantially greater variation with angular position than with distance alone. For example, at $r=50$\,km, the SINR ranges from $-7.40$\,dB when the receiver is oriented toward interferer I1 ($\theta=0^\circ$) to $+2.54$\,dB when positioned away from the constellation ($\theta=210^\circ$), corresponding to a 9.94\,dB spatial spread (Tables A.3a--d, Appendix). This pronounced spatial variation is driven by the constellation geometry rather than the landscape environment.

\subsubsection{Service Link (2~GHz): SINR Effects from Distance and Constellation Geometry}

The SINR degradation due to multi-HAPS interference at \textbf{2~GHz service links} is governed primarily by two factors: \textbf{(i)} the horizontal distance $r$ from the HAPS nadir, and \textbf{(ii)} the angular position of the receiver relative to the two interfering HAPS in the 65~km equilateral-triangle constellation (\S\ref{subsec:multinode-sinr}, Figure~\ref{fig:app-constellation-sectors}). The landscape environment does \emph{not} influence the SINR at this frequency, since both the desired and interfering links are modeled using free-space path loss (\S III.C; Arani et al.\ Eq.\ 2). Consequently, the suburban, urban, dense-urban, and high-rise environments produce identical SINR values, as confirmed numerically in Table~\ref{table:app-sinr-multinode-distances} and Figure~\ref{fig:multinode-interference}.

This simulation of 2~GHz service-link interference therefore demonstrates that distance alone is insufficient to characterize interference conditions and motivates the use of a DRL-based approach for adaptive scheduling.

\subsection{Scintillation Fading: Implementation and Results}
\label{subsec:scintillation}

Tropospheric scintillation (introduced in Section~III.B) is characterized in figures (Plots 9, 10, 11). This subsection is presented the the ITU-R P.618
fade-depth model that depends on empirical time-percentage statistics most meaningfully explained through parametric plots showing the dependencies on frequency, elevation angle,
and availability target. The deterministic fade-depth formula (Equation~\eqref{eq:scint_fade}) and stochastic fading envelope realizations are therefore grouped together with their
visualization and interpretation in the Results section for pedagogical clarity.

\textbf{Reference fade depths at K-band (20 GHz, $\theta=30^\circ$, per ITU-R P.618)}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $p=0.1\%$: $D_{\mathrm{ref}} \approx 13.14\,\mathrm{dB}$ (99.9\% availability target)
  \item $p=1.0\%$: $D_{\mathrm{ref}} \approx 10.73\,\mathrm{dB}$ (standard planning margin)
  \item $p=10.0\%$: $D_{\mathrm{ref}} \approx 7.59\,\mathrm{dB}$ (conservative)
\end{itemize}

These are \textbf{reference values at 20 GHz}. Actual fade depths for service (2 GHz) and feeder (38 GHz) links must be scaled using Equation~\eqref{eq:scint_fade} with the frequency factor $(f/f_{\mathrm{ref}})^{0.7}$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Service link (2 GHz)}: $(2/20)^{0.7} \approx 0.156$, so $D_{\mathrm{service}} \approx 0.156 \times D_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (lower fade depths, less severe fading)
  \item \textbf{Feeder link (38 GHz)}: $(38/20)^{0.7} \approx 1.50$, so $D_{\mathrm{feeder}} \approx 1.50 \times D_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (higher fade depths, more severe fading)
\end{itemize}

channels; Gamma fading (steep tail, severe events) models more extreme tropospheric
turbulence. Figure~\ref{fig:scint_timeseries} shows 1000 sample realizations at four
time percentages (0.1\%, 1\%, 5\%, 10\%), comparing both distributions against the
ITU P.618 fade-depth reference line. Both are used to explore sensitivity to fading
model assumptions.

\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Propagation metrics and path-loss characterisation.}
The four core propagation metrics---HAPS free-space path loss, UAV LoS probability,
average air-to-ground path loss, and single-node SINR---are implemented and
characterised through Figures~\ref{fig:fspl_comparison}--\ref{fig:los_pl_sinr}.

\paragraph{Multi-HAPS interference and landscape effects.}
The addition of multi-HAPS interference analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:multinode-interference}) characterizes realistic simultaneous-transmission
scenarios across all four landscape environments (suburban, urban, dense-urban, high-rise).
The analysis reveals that SINR behavior is landscape-dependent for \textbf{2 GHz service links}:
suburban areas maintain high LoS and sustain usable SINR to greater distances, while high-rise dense cities experience
sharp LoS collapse and interference-dominated regimes at short distances. Across all service-link landscapes,
simultaneous transmission from three HAPS nodes incurs a 10--20\,dB SINR penalty (verified in Figure~\ref{fig:multinode-interference}).

\noindent \textbf{Feeder link (38 GHz) does NOT experience this penalty}: The three interfering HAPS are $\approx 65$ km from the ground Gateway, placing them far outside the 10--500 m feeder coverage. Consequently, interference from constellation members remains well below thermal noise floor; feeder SINR is primarily noise-limited and approximately constant at $-7.2$~dB (Section~\ref{subsec:feeder-sinr}, Figure~\ref{fig:sinr-feeder-link}).

\paragraph{Scintillation fading (ITU P.618 implementation).}
Figures~\ref{fig:scint_fade_depth}--\ref{fig:scint_timeseries} introduce a physics-based model of tropospheric scintillation affecting both service links (UE $\leftrightarrow$ HAPS, 5$^\circ$--85$^\circ$ elevation-dependent) and feeder links (HAPS $\leftrightarrow$ Gateway, 25$^\circ$--45$^\circ$ fixed). The fade-depth model
\eqref{eq:scint_fade} accounts for frequency scaling ($\propto f^{0.7}$), elevation scaling
($\propto 1/\sin(\theta)$), and time-percentage statistics ($\propto \sqrt{\log(1/p)}$), directly from ITU-R P.618-13. Two distribution models (log-normal and Gamma) enable exploration of fading assumptions. Typical S-band (2~GHz) margins range 2.6--4.7\,dB across the 0.1\%--10\% time-percentage range, while Ka-band (38~GHz) margins range 7.6--13.1\,dB, reflecting the frequency dependence of tropospheric turbulence.

\noindent\textbf{Key Findings:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{9.94\,dB variation @ r~=~50\,km:} across the full 24-point angular sweep, SINR ranges --7.40\,dB (worst, $\theta=0^\circ$, toward I1) to +2.54\,dB (best, $\theta=210^\circ$)—vastly exceeds distance-based degradation. Note the asymmetric equilateral geometry (I1 @ $0^\circ$, I2 @ $60^\circ$) means the true best/worst angles are not the cardinal directions shown in Tables A.3a--d; see Figure~\ref{fig:app-constellation-sectors} for the good/bad sector layout.
\item \textbf{Spatial heterogeneity dominates r~$>$~30\,km:} At r~=~50\,km, 9.94\,dB spatial variation exceeds ~2--3\,dB per distance doubling.
\item \textbf{DRL scheduling necessity:} Distance-only assignment fails. Must learn angle-dependent interference patterns.
\item \textbf{Sector structure:} Good sector ($\sim$120--290$^\circ$, SINR~$>$~0\,dB); bad sector ($\sim$330--75$^\circ$, SINR~$<$~--5\,dB within roughly $345$--$60^\circ$).
\end{itemize}


\section{Phase 2: DRL for User Association}
% =====================================================================

Phase~2 introduces a Deep Q-Network (DQN) agent for dynamic user-to-platform association in the HAPS--UAV--Gateway network. Whereas Phase~1 characterized static link quality, Phase~2 learns real-time association decisions maximizing throughput, fairness, and energy efficiency.

\subsection{Motivation: Why DRL?}
\label{sec:phase2-motivation}

The spatial SINR heterogeneity discovered in Phase~1 (9.94\,dB variation at 50\,km driven by constellation geometry alone, Figure~\ref{fig:multinode-interference}) demonstrates that distance-only scheduling heuristics fail. A simple ``always pick max SINR'' rule cannot anticipate or adapt to the angular SINR patterns created by the 3-HAPS triangle. Moreover, when weather degrades feeder links (Section~\ref{subsec:multinode-sinr}), the network must dynamically fail-over users from one platform to another while respecting battery constraints on each HAPS node.

DRL offers a principled approach: the agent observes the current network state (SINR to each platform, battery levels, weather forecast) and learns a policy $\pi(a | s)$ that selects the best platform for each user, maximizing cumulative reward. By training on diverse scenarios (rain events, user mobility, multi-node interference), the learned policy discovers non-obvious patterns (e.g., preemptively rerouting from a well-positioned but rain-threatened link to a less ideal but stable backup) that no hand-crafted rule would capture.

\subsection{Per-User Markov Decision Process (MDP) Formulation}

The model has 4 platform choices (HAPS\_1, HAPS\_2, HAPS\_3, UAV\_relay). With N users, a centralized MDP would face $4^N$ possible joint assignments—intractable for large networks. We instead decompose into per-user MDPs: each user independently chooses a platform, and the network computes cumulative reward after all assignments.

\subsubsection{State Space}

The state vector for user~$k$ is 12-dimensional:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{s}_k &= [\text{SINR}_{k,1,2,3}, r_{k,1,2,3}, \text{SoC}_{1,2,3},\nonumber\\
&\quad R_{\text{rain}}, V_{\text{vis}}, t_{\text{hour}} ]
\label{eq:state-vector}
\end{align}

\noindent \textbf{Feature Definitions}
(cross-referenced to Phase~1 results):

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item $\text{SINR}_{k,p}$: Downlink SINR at user $k$ from platform $p$ (dB). Phase~1 measured $[-7.4, +7.3]$~dB across 100~km footprint (Fig.~\ref{fig:multinode-interference}); bounds $[-20, +30]$~dB provide headroom for edge cases.
  \item $r_{k,p}$: Horizontal distance user~$k$ to platform~$p$ (km). Range $[0, 100]$~km covers service footprint (§~II).
  \item $\text{SoC}_p$: Battery state-of-charge platform~$p$ (\%). Observed by all users; range $[0, 100]\,\%$ (empty to full).
  \item $R_{\text{rain}}$: Rainfall rate (mm/h). Range $[0, 50]$~mm/h (rare events).
  \item $V_{\text{vis}}$: Visibility (m). Range $[0, 10]$~km (fog/clear).
  \item $t_{\text{hour}}$: Time-of-day (normalized). Range $[0, 1]$ maps 0~$\to$~midnight, 0.5~$\to$~noon.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Observation Space Bounds} (negative SINR preserved for outage signals):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S} = \underbrace{[-20,+30]^3}_{\text{SINR}_{k,p}} \times \underbrace{[0,100]^3}_{r_{k,p}\text{ (km)}} \times \underbrace{[0,100]^3}_{\text{SoC}_p\text{ (\%)}} \times \underbrace{[0,50]}_{\text{rain}} \times \underbrace{[0,10k]}_{\text{vis (m)}} \times \underbrace{[0,1]}_{t}
\label{eq:obs-space}
\end{equation}

\noindent \textbf{Phase 2 DQN Scope} (scintillation deferred to Phase 3):
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item \textbf{Included}: Deterministic SINR from constellation geometry (Phase~1); rainfall \& visibility for feeder margin planning; battery state for load balancing
  \item \textbf{NOT included}: Tropospheric scintillation fading (Section~\ref{subsec:scintillation}) --- time-varying random process requiring LSTM forecasting (Phase~3)
  \item \textbf{Approximation}: Phase~2 assumes worst-case static fading margin ($3.65$~dB service, $10.53$~dB feeder at $p=1\%$) or uses Phase~1 fade-depth statistics as penalty modulation
  \item \textbf{Phase 3}: LSTM weather forecaster will predict scintillation fading from rainfall/visibility timeseries, enabling adaptive margin reallocation
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Validation:} Phase~1 measured SINR $\in [-7.40, +7.30]$~dB over 100~km (Table A.3, worst/best-case constellation angles). Bounds $[-20, +30]$ dB provide $\pm 12.6$~dB margin for edge cases (atmospheric fading, relay paths in v2).

\subsubsection{Action Space}

Discrete platform choice: $\mathbf{a}_k \in \{0, 1, 2\}$ (HAPS1, HAPS2, HAPS3). UAV relay ($a=3$) deferred to Phase~2 v2.

\subsubsection{Reward Function}

All computations use \textbf{linear SINR} ($\gamma_k = 10^{\text{SINR}_{\text{dB}}/10}$), never dB arithmetic:
\begin{align}
R(\mathbf{a}) &= 0.5 L_{\text{agg}} + 0.3 J_{\text{fair}} - 0.2 P_{\text{eng}} + \text{Penalties}\\
L_{\text{agg}} &= \sum_{k=1}^N \log_2(1 + \gamma_k) \quad [\text{bits/s/Hz}]\\
J_{\text{fair}} &= \frac{(\sum_k \gamma_k)^2}{N \sum_k \gamma_k^2} \in [0,1]\\
P_{\text{eng}} &= \frac{1}{3}\sum_{p=1}^{3} \left(1 - \frac{\text{SoC}_p}{100}\right)
\end{align}

\noindent \textbf{Penalties} (thresholds calibrated to Phase~1 measured ranges; aggregated as network average, not per-user):
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item Severe outage: $-100 \times (\text{fraction of users in outage})$ if $\text{SINR}_{k,\text{dB}} < -2$~dB (well below average +2~dB)
  \item Degraded service: $-20 \times (\text{fraction of users degraded})$ if $-2 \le \text{SINR}_{k,\text{dB}} < 0$~dB (below average)
  \item Low-battery: $-50$ if $\text{SoC}_p < 10\,\%$ with $> 0$ assigned users
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{Threshold Rationale:} Phase~1 measured: best $+7.3$~dB, average $+2.0$~dB, worst $-4.3$~dB. Penalties trigger below average ($< 0$~dB), not within normal operating range ($+2$ to $+7$~dB), allowing agent to reward good performance ($> +2$~dB) without false penalties.

\subsection{Critical Implementation Fixes}

Phase~2 training must avoid six silent errors:

\textbf{Error 1:} Jain's index on dB (wrong---log scale)
\begin{equation}
J_{\text{wrong}} = \frac{(\sum_k \text{SINR}_{k,\text{dB}})^2}{N \sum_k \text{SINR}_{k,\text{dB}}^2}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Fix:} Use linear scale
\begin{equation}
J_{\text{correct}} = \frac{(\sum_k 10^{\text{SINR}_k/10})^2}{N \sum_k (10^{\text{SINR}_k/10})^2}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Error 2:} SINR bounds $[0,100]$ hide outages.
\textbf{Fix:} Use $\text{SINR} \in [-20,+30]$~dB.

\textbf{Error 3:} HAPS solar from ground rain (HAPS at 20~km is above clouds).
\textbf{Fix:} Use diurnal cycle only, not weather.

\textbf{Error 4:} LSTM $(24,2) \to (60,)$ resolution mismatch.
\textbf{Fix:} Match resolution: $(24,2) \to (1,2)$ (hourly to hourly).

\textbf{Error 5:} Reward broadcast to all $N$ users identically (the fully-aggregated
network reward $R(\mathbf{a})$ given to every user's transition, regardless of that
user's own action). With $N=50$ simultaneously-acting users, any single user's choice
moves $R(\mathbf{a})$ by only a small fraction of its value, so the DQN cannot tell
which action caused the change -- training collapsed to a constant always-pick-HAPS-1
policy (identical evaluation reward/fairness/outage across 200+ consecutive training
episodes; verified by inspecting the learned policy's action distribution directly).
\textbf{Fix:} Decompose the additive components of $R(\mathbf{a})$ -- $L_{\text{agg}}$
and the outage penalties are literally sums (resp. fractions) over users -- into a
per-user share, so each user is credited with its own throughput/outage contribution.
$J_{\text{fair}}$ and $P_{\text{eng}}$ are genuinely network-level and remain a shared
term. This does not change the total reward mass, only its attribution for learning.

\textbf{Error 6:} HAPS battery SoC update treats a dimensionless energy fraction as if
it were already a percentage: $\text{SoC} \mathrel{+}= (\Delta E_{\text{Wh}} / C_{\text{battery}})$
instead of $\text{SoC} \mathrel{+}= 100 \times (\Delta E_{\text{Wh}} / C_{\text{battery}})$.
This under-scales every SoC update by $100\times$, so battery state barely moves
regardless of policy (observed: SoC pinned at $\approx$65\% across all three
Section~\ref{sec:phase2-results} policies before the fix) -- silently disabling the
entire energy/battery term of the reward. \textbf{Fix:} apply the missing $\times100$
conversion, and use $C_{\text{battery}}=12{,}000$~Wh matching Table~\ref{tab:config}'s
value (the module's own uncorrected default, 1000~Wh, is 12$\times$ too small and
produces implausible full-charge/full-drain swings within a single hour).

\subsection{HAPS Battery Model}

\subsubsection{Energy Balance}

State-of-charge updated hourly:
\begin{equation}
\Delta \text{SoC} = \frac{P_{\text{solar}}(t) - (P_{\text{feeder}} + P_{\text{payload}} + \sum_k P_{k,\text{user}})}{C_{\text{battery}}}
\label{eq:battery-balance}
\end{equation}

where: $P_{\text{feeder}} = 500$~W (transmitter), $P_{\text{payload}} = 200$~W (control), $P_{k,\text{user}}$ (assigned load), $C_{\text{battery}} = 12000$~Wh.

\subsubsection{Solar Power (Diurnal Cycle)}

\textbf{Critical principle:} HAPS at 20~km (stratosphere, above $>99\%$ clouds). Solar independent of ground weather.

\noindent Solar irradiance (cosine profile):
\begin{equation}
I_0(t_{\text{hr}}) = \begin{cases}
600 \cos(\pi(t_{\text{hr}} - 12)/12) & 6 \le t_{\text{hr}} \le 18 \\
0 & \text{night}
\end{cases} \quad [\text{W/m}^2]
\end{equation}

\noindent Generated power:
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{solar}}(t) = 20\,\text{m}^2 \times 0.20 \times I_0(t) = 2400\,\text{W (noon)}, \, 0\,\text{W (night)}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Operating Thresholds}

SoC clipped to $[0, 100]\,\%$:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
  \item \textbf{Charging:} Day (6 AM--6 PM) when $P_{\text{solar}} > P_{\text{load}}$
  \item \textbf{Draining:} Night when $P_{\text{solar}} = 0$
  \item \textbf{Critical:} SoC $< 5\,\%$ $\Rightarrow$ shutdown (no service)
  \item \textbf{Penalty:} SoC $< 10\,\%$ with users $\Rightarrow$ reward $-50$ (load balancing)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Thresholds:} SoC $\in [0,100]\,\%$. Critical: $< 5\%$ (shutdown); penalty: $< 10\%$ (overload). Agent learns to balance users across platforms to avoid over-draining during night.
