Phase 2: DQN weather-parameter training and validation guide

Purpose
- Train a DQN policy that learns channel and movement actions under changing weather conditions.
- Use weather-driven profiling as a dynamic control problem instead of a purely static equation-based evaluation.
- Validate whether the learned policy improves fairness, throughput, and outage performance relative to non-learning baselines.

Repository entry points
- DQN agent: src/drl_dqn/dqn_agent.py
- Environment: src/drl_dqn/haps_env.py
- Weather generator: src/drl_dqn/weather_scenario_generator.py
- Training script: src/drl_dqn/train_dqn_phase2.py
- Reward logic: src/drl_dqn/reward_function.py

1. Weather parameters used in training

The DQN does not optimize a fixed equation directly; it learns from a weather-conditioned state vector. Each episode samples a weather scenario, and the environment keeps it fixed for the episode.

Weather dimensions used by the environment:
- Rain rate: 0, 2, 5, 10, 20 mm/h
- Visibility: 15, 7.5, 2.5, 0.5 km
- Hour of day: 0–24 h
- Derived quantities: fog/liquid water, humidity, atmospheric pressure, temperature

These are generated by WeatherScenarioGenerator in src/drl_dqn/weather_scenario_generator.py.

The generator supports three modes:
- random_mix: default for training; uniform random mixing across weather profiles
- fixed: use one repeated scenario for debugging and reproducibility
- deterministic: cycle through predefined composite weather scenarios

2. Why weather is included in DQN state

The DQN action policy should respond to:
- rain-induced attenuation
- visibility degradation / fog effects
- diurnal variation that changes propagation and environmental stress
- fairness and outage risk under varying background conditions

A pure equation-based model can compute instantaneous SINR or path loss under a fixed assumption. It cannot learn a control policy that changes actions online to minimize outage and maintain fairness across a dynamically changing environment.

5. How to decide whether the training is good enough

Stop training when the following are stable across several evaluation checkpoints:
- eval_reward no longer improves materially
- fairness remains near a high value (close to 1.0 is ideal)
- outage count stops decreasing meaningfully
- the policy remains stable across weather scenarios

A healthy first-pass run is one where:
- evaluation reward is positive and increasing early on
- fairness remains high
- outage does not explode
- logs and plots are produced without errors

6. Validation against no-DRL baselines

To claim DQN is useful, it must be shown to outperform a baseline policy under identical conditions.

Recommended baselines:
- random action policy
- fixed channel/motion policy
- greedy SINR-maximizing policy

Validation procedure:
1. Use the same number of UAVs, users, channels, and weather profiles.
2. Use the same held-out evaluation seeds.
3. Evaluate each policy for the same number of episodes.
4. Measure:
   - average reward
   - Jain fairness index
   - outage count / served fraction
   - throughput if relevant
5. Compare mean values and variation across multiple seeds.

A DQN policy is selected only if it consistently outperforms the baseline on the task objective.

7. Why DQN is better than a purely mathematical equation-based analysis

A mathematical model is necessary for physics and propagation analysis, but it is not sufficient for decision-making under uncertainty.

Equation-based analysis gives:
- instantaneous path loss
- SINR for a fixed geometry or fixed weather state
- average behavior under simplified assumptions

It does not learn how to adapt actions online when conditions change. In particular, the DQN can:
- choose UAV movement actions in response to user distribution and interference
- select channels to reduce interference and improve fairness
- react to evolving weather states during the episode
- balance multiple objectives such as fairness, outage, and served demand

This is a closed-loop control problem, not just a static link-budget problem. A DQN learns a policy that maps weather-conditioned state to action, which is the step missing from pure equation-driven analysis.

This distinction matters in Phase 2:
- the mathematical model explains what the wireless system should do physically
- the DQN determines what policy should be chosen under variable conditions to maximize network utility in practice

In short, the equation-based model tells us the physics; the DQN tells us the control policy.

8. Final conclusion for Phase 2

The DQN-based Phase 2 analysis provides a more realistic and operational view of the HAPS-UAV network than a static mathematical model alone because it incorporates dynamic weather variations, fairness tradeoffs, and online action selection. The resulting policy is not simply a formula evaluation; it is a learning-based controller that adapts to the environment.

For Phase 2, the recommended narrative is:
- physics equations define the channel and propagation behavior
- weather parameters define the dynamic operating conditions
- the DQN learns an adaptive scheduling policy under those conditions
- this policy is validated against non-learning baselines to establish its practical advantage
